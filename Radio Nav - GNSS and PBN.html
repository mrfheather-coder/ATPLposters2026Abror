Part I · Errors & Integrity

Of Satellites & Their Discontents

Six pseudorange errors, two integrity algorithms, and an ionosphere that refuses to behave. Everything the IAA will ask about GNSS reliability sits in this section.

§ 1.01The Pseudorange Error Budget

Standard EASA / ICAO Error Decomposition

Pseudorange is a range measurement that lies. The signal leaves the satellite at a known time and arrives at the receiver having passed through the thickest part of the atmosphere, bounced off wing surfaces, and been timed by a clock that drifts. The pilot does not need to fix any of this in flight — but the examiner wants you to know what each error is, which dominates, and what removes it.

Error SourceMagnitude · Correction
SourceTypical SizePrimary Correction
Ionospheric delayLargest single unaided errorDual-frequency OR Klobuchar model OR SBAS
Tropospheric delayA few metresModelled (Hopfield / Saastamoinen) onboard
Ephemeris / orbit errorLow-metreSBAS long-term corrections
Satellite clock errorSub-metre to metreBroadcast correction in nav msg; SBAS
Receiver noise & clockSub-metreNot externally correctable
MultipathSite-dependent; small airborneAntenna design, signal masking

The single largest unaided error is ionospheric delay, and it is frequency-dependent — which is exactly what dual-frequency receivers exploit.

IAA phrasing: "Which GNSS error cannot be corrected by an augmentation system?"

Receiver noise. It is generated inside the receiver; no external signal can fix it. SBAS/GBAS corrections handle ionospheric, ephemeris, and satellite clock errors — not the noise floor of your own hardware.

§ 1.02RAIM, FDE, and Counting Satellites

Receiver Autonomous Integrity Monitoring SKYBRARY · ICAO

Integrity is not accuracy. A GNSS position can be accurate and still have no integrity — the number is right, but the receiver has no way to verify it. RAIM is the onboard algorithm that answers: is this position trustworthy right now? The answer depends entirely on how many satellites the receiver is currently tracking.

4 satellites
Minimum nav fix
  • 3D position + time
  • No RAIM possible
  • No integrity monitoring
  • Fix only
5 satellites
RAIM · Detection
  • Can detect a faulty SV
  • Cannot identify which one
  • Annunciates: integrity failure
6 satellites
FDE · Detection & Exclusion
  • Fault Detection & Exclusion
  • Identifies & removes bad SV
  • Navigation continues
  • Required for some oceanic RNP ops

Five to detect. Six to exclude.

A RAIM prediction check is normally made before the relevant flight phase — before dispatch for an RNP APCH or before the oceanic entry point — either by an AIS prediction service, the FMS, or an operator-provided tool. The check establishes that RAIM will be available at the planned ETA. This is examinable and the IAA likes to ask it.

§ 1.03Why Dual-Frequency Solves the Ionosphere

A Matter of Physics, Not Modelling

Ionospheric delay is inversely proportional to frequency squared. Because L1 and L5 (or L1 and L2) see different delays for the same signal path, a receiver that measures both can solve for the actual delay directly — rather than modelling it.

Klobuchar Model
Single-Frequency Correction · Broadcast in Nav Msg

Removes roughly half of the ionospheric error for single-frequency receivers.

Dual-Frequency
Direct Measurement

Near-complete removal. The method of choice where available. Modern airborne receivers with DFMC (dual-frequency multi-constellation) use this.

Part II · Augmentation Systems

Three Ways to Trust the Signal

ABAS happens inside the aircraft. SBAS happens from space. GBAS happens at the airport. Each is a different answer to the same problem: how do we prove the satellites aren't lying?

§ 2.01The Hierarchy

ABAS · SBAS · GBAS ICAO ANNEX 10 · FAA
SystemScope
System Full name Method Coverage Example
ABAS Aircraft-Based Augmentation Onboard only — RAIM, FDE, baro-aiding, IRS hybridisation Worldwide RAIM
SBAS Satellite-Based Augmentation Ground stations compute corrections; geostationary satellites broadcast on L1 Continental EGNOS · WAAS · MSAS · GAGAN
GBAS Ground-Based Augmentation Airport reference receivers broadcast differential corrections via VDB (VHF data broadcast) ~23 NM from reference GLS approaches (CAT I, extending to CAT III)

"The nominal service range of a GBAS installation is approximately..."

23 nautical miles from the reference point. Confirmed by FAA, ESA Navipedia, and ICAO SARPS documents. GBAS is deliberately local — that is how it achieves the integrity required for precision approach.

§ 2.02SBAS — EGNOS in Practice

European Geostationary Navigation Overlay Service

EGNOS gives the European pilot LPV approach minima down to roughly 200 ft without an ILS. It does three things: corrects GPS errors, monitors satellite integrity, and publishes warnings.

The SBAS signal is broadcast on the same L1 frequency as GPS itself. Any GPS receiver with SBAS logic can pick up the corrections in parallel with the nav signal — no second antenna required.

EGNOS ServicesThree Levels
Open ServiceFree, mass-market use — navigation, surveying, agriculture
Safety-of-LifeAviation use — LPV, LP, LNAV/VNAV minima
EDASEGNOS Data Access Service — commercial ground-based access

§ 2.03GBAS — The Local Operator

VDB Data Link · GLS Approaches FAA · ICAO SARPS

GBAS provides local differential corrections. The GLS (GBAS Landing System) approach displays on an ILS-style indicator, but the underlying fix is GPS, corrected by airport-local reference receivers.

VDB — VHF Data Broadcast
108.000 – 117.975 MHz

The GBAS broadcast occupies a VHF slot in the same band as ILS localiser / VOR. The aircraft tunes a GLS channel number (not a frequency directly) and the avionics decode the differential data.

GAST
GBAS Approach Service Type

GAST-C supports CAT I. GAST-D supports CAT III (SARPS effective 2018; operational deployment ongoing).

Part III · Technical Core

The Hardware Nobody Loves

Codes, encryption, jammers, spoofers. The technical bits of GNSS that live quietly in the question bank.

§ 3.01CDMA vs FDMA

How Four Constellations Share the Sky

GPS, Galileo, and BeiDou all use CDMA — Code Division Multiple Access. Every satellite transmits on the same carrier (L1), but each uses a unique pseudo-random code. The receiver separates them by code, not by frequency.

GLONASS historically used FDMA — Frequency Division Multiple Access — with each satellite on its own frequency slot. Modern GLONASS satellites also broadcast CDMA signals for interoperability, but classic GLONASS receivers still handle FDMA.

"Which core GNSS constellation historically uses FDMA?"

GLONASS. All others are CDMA.

§ 3.02Selective Availability & Anti-Spoofing

One Retired · One Still Running

Selective Availability (SA) was deliberate error introduction by the US DoD to degrade civilian accuracy. It was deactivated on 1 May 2000, but the question bank has a long memory. If a question mentions SA, the answer is almost always: no longer operative; civil accuracy improved from ~100 m to ~10 m on deactivation.

Anti-Spoofing (A-S)
P-code → Y-code encryption

Unlike SA, A-S is still active. It encrypts the precise (P) code into the Y code, accessible only to authorised military users via crypto keys. Civilian users are unaffected — they use the C/A code.

§ 3.03Jamming, Spoofing, and Modern Threats

Eastern Mediterranean · Baltic · Increasingly Examinable
Jamming
Denial by noise
  • Overwhelms receiver with RF noise
  • Receiver loses lock on GNSS
  • FMS reverts to DME/DME/IRS
  • Detectable — receiver knows
Spoofing
Deception by false signal
  • Broadcasts fake GNSS signals
  • Receiver computes wrong but plausible position
  • No loss-of-signal alarm
  • More dangerous than jamming
Mitigations
Aircraft response
  • Multi-constellation receivers
  • IRS hybridisation (reasonability)
  • Crew awareness via NOTAMs
  • Cross-check DME, VOR, radar

"What is the key difference between GNSS jamming and spoofing?"

Jamming denies the signal — the receiver knows it has lost GPS. Spoofing provides a false signal — the receiver believes a wrong position is correct. Spoofing is the more dangerous of the two because there is no warning to the crew.

Ten Specs, One Distinction

RNP is RNAV with onboard performance monitoring and alerting. That is the entire distinction.

The on-board performance monitoring and alerting — OBPMA — is the defining feature of the RNP family. An RNP system must monitor whether it is meeting the required accuracy and, if it cannot, alert the flight crew. An RNAV system has no such self-check. This is the one-line examinable difference.

§ 4.02The 95% Rule

Accuracy, Formally Stated PBN PORTAL · ICAO

When a specification says "RNAV 1," it means: the aircraft is required to maintain a total lateral navigation error within ±1 NM for 95% of the total flight time. This is a 2σ (two-sigma) confidence level.

Containment (for RNP)
2× the Accuracy Value

The RNP system must alert the crew if navigation error exceeds twice the specified accuracy. For RNP 1, alerting threshold is 2 NM. This is OBPMA in action.

§ 4.03A-RNP — What It Covers

Advanced RNP Scope

Advanced RNP is the "all phases" spec. A single A-RNP approval lets an operator conduct operations under a family of specifications without needing each one separately:

RNAV 5Included
RNAV 2 / RNAV 1Included
RNP 2 / RNP 1Included
RNP APCHIncluded (non-AR)
RNP AR APCHExcluded — separate Specific Approval required

Doc 9613 5th Edition clarification: the Final Approach Segment is no longer considered part of A-RNP; scalability is replaced by the use of RNP 0.3 NM outside the FAS.

"Which nav spec requires a Specific Approval rather than a standard approval?"

RNP AR APCH (and RNP AR DP, recently added in Doc 9613 5th Edition). RNP 0.3 for helicopters also requires separate approval.

Part V · Total System Error

Three Errors, One Sum

TSE = PDE + NSE + FTE. The IAA asks this more often than any other PBN question. Commit it to reflex.

§ 5.01TSE = PDE + NSE + FTE

The Defining Equation of PBN EASA · EUROCONTROL PBN PORTAL

Total System Error is the difference between the aircraft's actual position and the desired position along the intended flight path. Three independent components sum to it.

ComponentsMeaning and Values
ComponentMeaningTypical / required value
PDE Path Definition Error — difference between the path defined in the navigation database and the intended path Assumed zero (database integrity)
NSE Navigation System Error — difference between FMS-computed position and actual position (the sensor error) Depends on sensor suite
FTE Flight Technical Error — difference between FMS-computed position and the desired path (pilot / autopilot tracking error) ≤ ½ RNP value (see right)

PDE is assumed zero. FTE must be kept to no more than half the RNP value.

For an RNP APCH, the maximum FTE is 0.5 NM for the initial, intermediate, and missed approach segments, and 0.15 NM for the final approach segment. These limits are published in the EASA CS-ACNS airworthiness rules and referenced in operator advisory circulars. If the FMS shows a cross-track error exceeding half the applicable RNP, the approach must be discontinued.

"Which component of Total System Error is assumed to be zero?"

Path Definition Error (PDE). This assumption depends on navigation database integrity — which is why AIRAC validity and database management are examinable.

Part VI · Approach Operations

Four Minima, One Plate

LNAV, LNAV/VNAV, LP, LPV — four lines of minima, each with its own equipment requirements. Read the chart correctly and the decision is made for you.

§ 6.01RNP APCH — The Four Minima

Reading the Chart FAA AIM · SKYBRARY
LNAV
Lateral · Non-Precision
  • 2D approach, no vertical guidance
  • Pilot flies step-down
  • MDA — minimum descent altitude
  • Baseline GNSS capability (no SBAS required)
LNAV/VNAV
Lateral + Baro-VNAV
  • Vertical from baro-VNAV or SBAS
  • Temperature-corrected (baro path)
  • DA — decision altitude
  • Temperature restrictions on chart
LP & LPV
SBAS Required
  • LP — localiser performance, lateral only (MDA)
  • LPV — localiser performance with vertical (DA)
  • ILS-like minima for LPV, often down to ~200 ft
  • Pilot must use SBAS to fly to LP or LPV minima

"Which RNP APCH minima require SBAS?"

LP and LPV. LNAV uses unaugmented GNSS (RAIM). LNAV/VNAV typically uses baro-VNAV for the vertical component. Confirmed by the FAA AIM explicitly: "Pilots are required to use SBAS to fly to the LPV or LP minima."

§ 6.02RNP AR — Authorisation Required

0.1 NM Accuracy · RF Legs · Specific Approval

RNP AR approaches are used in terrain-challenging airports. They allow curved path segments (RF legs), reduced obstacle assessment, and final segments as tight as ±0.1 NM.

The "AR" is operational, not technical: the operator must be specifically authorised, aircrew must be specifically trained, and the aircraft must be specifically equipped and approved. This is why A-RNP does not automatically cover RNP AR — the approval chain is separate.

RF — Radius-to-Fix
Curved Path Segment

A constant-radius curved segment between two fixes. Flown by the FMS at constant bank, not by intercepting a straight leg. Mandatory for RNP AR; optional for RNP APCH; required functionality for A-RNP.

§ 6.03Baro-VNAV & Cold Temperatures

The Cold Day Trap

Baro-VNAV uses altimetry for vertical guidance. In cold temperatures, true altitude is lower than indicated altitude, eroding obstacle clearance on a baro-coded path.

Approach charts publish a temperature limit for baro-LNAV/VNAV operations (e.g. "LNAV/VNAV NA below −15°C"). Below that temperature, either LNAV minima must be used, or the approach is not flown. SBAS VNAV (where available on LNAV/VNAV or as LPV) does not suffer from this limitation because vertical comes from geometry, not air pressure.

Part VII · Operational Side

AIRAC, Databases, and the Clock

PBN lives on valid data. The navigation database cycle is the clock that keeps everything honest.

§ 7.01The AIRAC Cycle

28 Days · Thursday Effective · 00:00 UTC

AIRAC — Aeronautical Information Regulation And Control — is the global schedule synchronising navigation database updates worldwide. Every 28 days, a new cycle takes effect at 00:00 UTC on a Thursday.

The Three AIRAC NumbersCommit to Memory
28 days Cycle duration — the period between successive AIRAC effective dates.
42 days Notification — states must notify AIS providers of changes at least 42 days before effective date (ICAO Annex 15 standard).
56 days Publication — charts and data to be published at least 56 days before effective date.

An aircraft operating RNP approaches must have a current navigation database. Using expired AIRAC data for an RNP APCH is an operational no-go — it is not a paperwork technicality.

"What is the duration of one AIRAC cycle?"

28 days. Not 30. Not a month. Exactly 28.

Part VIII · Trap Room

Where Marks Go to Die

Drill these until the answers are reflex. Each is built from source-verified material; none is a reproduced IAA question, but the phrasings mirror standard EASA exam patterns.

Q1. A GNSS receiver tracking 5 satellites provides what level of integrity?

RAIM fault detection only. Cannot identify which satellite is faulty. 6 satellites required for Fault Detection and Exclusion (FDE).

Q2. In the Total System Error formula, which component is assumed to be zero?

Path Definition Error (PDE). The assumption relies on navigation database integrity.

Q3. The maximum allowable Flight Technical Error for the final segment of an RNP approach is...

0.15 NM for the final approach segment; 0.5 NM for initial / intermediate / missed segments.

Q4. Duration of one AIRAC cycle?

28 days. Publication minimum 56 days before effective date; notification minimum 42 days.

Q5. Which RNP APCH minima require SBAS?

LP and LPV. (FAA AIM explicit: "Pilots are required to use SBAS to fly to the LPV or LP minima.") LNAV uses unaugmented GNSS. LNAV/VNAV typically uses baro-VNAV for vertical.

Q6. The nominal service range of GBAS is approximately...

23 nautical miles from the reference point. Verified against FAA technical documentation and ICAO SARPS (Annex 10).

Q7. Which navigation specifications are covered by an A-RNP approval?

RNAV 5, RNAV 2, RNAV 1, RNP 2, RNP 1, RNP APCH. RNP AR APCH is NOT included — it requires separate Specific Approval.

Q8. The single defining distinction between an RNAV and an RNP specification is...

On-Board Performance Monitoring and Alerting (OBPMA). RNP specs require it; RNAV specs do not. Accuracy values overlap (e.g. RNAV 1 and RNP 1 both ±1 NM) — OBPMA is the true differentiator.

Q9. Which GNSS error cannot be corrected by an augmentation system?

Receiver noise. Generated inside the receiver; no external signal can fix it.

Q10. Most effective method for correcting ionospheric delay?

Dual-frequency measurement. The Klobuchar single-frequency model removes roughly half the error; dual-frequency exploits the physics directly (frequency-squared dependence) and removes nearly all of it.

Q11. The operational difference between GNSS jamming and spoofing is...

Jamming denies the signal — receiver knows it has lost GNSS. Spoofing provides false signals — receiver believes a wrong position is valid. Spoofing is more dangerous because there is no warning to the crew.

Q12. Which core GNSS constellation historically uses FDMA rather than CDMA?

GLONASS. (Modern GLONASS satellites additionally broadcast CDMA signals for interoperability, but the classic GLONASS architecture is FDMA.) GPS, Galileo, and BeiDou all use CDMA.

Q13. How many navigation specifications are identified in ICAO Doc 9613 (5th Edition)?

Ten — three RNAV (RNAV 10, RNAV 5, RNAV 2, RNAV 1 counting as four if RNAV 10 is classed as RNAV; otherwise three RNAV + seven RNP) — conventional count is 3 RNAV + 7 RNP = 10. RNP 0.3 is helicopter-specific and is often the distractor.

Q14. The RNP 0.3 navigation specification is intended for...

Helicopter operations (Doc 9613 5th Edition clarified it is solely for helicopter use). Covers en-route continental, arrival, departure, and approach — excluding the final approach segment.

Q15. What is the GBAS VHF Data Broadcast frequency band?

108.000 – 117.975 MHz — the same VHF band as ILS localisers and VORs. Channel spacing requirements and coordination with other VHF services apply (per ICAO Doc 9718).
