Sign Convention — Critical to Remember!

The datum is a reference point (often the nose or firewall). Everything is measured from here. Items aft (behind) the datum have positive arms. Items forward (ahead) of the datum have negative arms.

✏️ WORKED EXAMPLE

Question

An aircraft has a nose gear (forward of main gear datum) carrying 4,350 kg at -12.2m, and two main gears carrying 15,005 kg each at 0m. Find the CG position.

Nose gear: 4,350 kg @ -12.2m
Main gears: 2 × 15,005 kg @ 0m
1
Calculate total mass
4,350 + (2 × 15,005) = 34,360 kg
2
Calculate total moment
(4,350 × -12.2) + (30,010 × 0) = -53,070 kg·m
3
Calculate CG position
CG = -53,070 ÷ 34,360 = -1.54m
ANSWER
CG is 1.54m FORWARD of the main gear

03
CG Expression
Percentage of MAC
%MAC = ((L − LEMAC) ÷ MAC) × 100
Expressing CG position as a percentage along the wing chord
L
CG Position
Distance from datum to the Centre of Gravity
LEMAC
Leading Edge of MAC
Distance from datum to where MAC starts
MAC
Mean Aerodynamic Chord
The length of the "average" wing chord
Why use %MAC instead of just distance?

On swept and tapered wings, the chord length varies across the span. The MAC is a single representative chord that aerodynamically represents the whole wing. Expressing CG as %MAC standardizes the measurement — typical limits are around 15% to 35% MAC. Too far forward = nose heavy. Too far aft = unstable.

DATUM
LEMAC
← MAC Length →
CG (L)
← L (Datum to CG) →

✏️ WORKED EXAMPLE

Question

The CG is located 15 meters from the datum. LEMAC is at 12 meters, and MAC length is 6 meters. What is the CG as %MAC?

L (CG): 15 m
LEMAC: 12 m
MAC: 6 m
1
Find distance into MAC
L − LEMAC = 15 − 12 = 3 m
2
Divide by MAC length and multiply by 100
(3 ÷ 6) × 100 = 50%
ANSWER
CG is at 50% MAC
04
Moving Existing Mass
CG Shift Formula
ΔCG = (m × d) ÷ M
How much the CG moves when you reposition existing mass
ΔCG
Change in CG
How far the CG moves (same units as d)
m
Mass Moved
The mass being repositioned
d
Distance Moved
How far the mass travels
M
Total Mass
Aircraft's total mass (doesn't change)
When to use this formula

Use this when moving existing mass from one location to another — like when ground crew loads cargo in the wrong hold, or passengers change seats. The total aircraft mass stays the same; only the CG position changes. CG moves in the same direction as the mass moves.

⚠️
Direction rule: If mass moves AFT → CG moves AFT. If mass moves FORWARD → CG moves FORWARD.

✏️ WORKED EXAMPLE

Question

An aircraft has a TOM of 57,600 kg and CG at 646 inches. Ground crew loaded 900 kg of baggage into the rear hold instead of the front. The holds are 517 inches apart. Is the new CG within limits (640-661 inches)?

TOM: 57,600 kg
Current CG: 646 in
Mass moved: 900 kg
Distance: 517 in
Limits: 640–661 in
1
Apply the formula
ΔCG = (900 × 517) ÷ 57,600
2
Calculate
ΔCG = 465,300 ÷ 57,600 = 8.08 inches
3
Mass moved aft, so CG moves aft
New CG = 646 + 8 = 654 inches
4
Check limits: 640 < 654 < 661
654 is WITHIN LIMITS ✓
ANSWER
Yes, new CG (654 in) is within limits

Adding Mass to Shift CG
Ballast Calculation

How much ballast to add to bring CG within limits

M

Arm Distance
From target CG to ballast location
What is ballast?

Ballast is dead weight (lead, steel, sand) added purely to shift the CG when it's out of limits and you can't rearrange the existing load. It's a last resort — ballast takes up useful payload capacity. To move CG forward, add ballast forward. To move CG aft, add ballast aft.

⚠️
Important: Use distance from target CG to ballast position, not from current CG. This accounts for the mass being added.

✏️ WORKED EXAMPLE

Question

Aircraft has TOM of 3,800 lb with CG at 95 inches — outside the rear limit of 93 inches. How much ballast in the forward cargo hold (at 22.5 inches) will bring CG to 93 inches?

TOM: 3,800 lb
Current CG: 95 in
Target CG: 93 in
Ballast arm: 22.5 in
1
Find required CG shift
ΔCG = 95 − 93 = 2 inches forward
2
Find distance from target CG to ballast
d = 93 − 22.5 = 70.5 inches
3
Apply the formula
Mass = (2 × 3,800) ÷ 70.5 = 7,600 ÷ 70.5 = 107.8 lb
ANSWER
≈ 108 lb of ballast in forward cargo
06
Adding or Removing Mass
The Proportion Method
Two versions — choose based on what you know

When adding or removing mass (not just moving it), the total mass changes. Use these proportion formulas depending on whether you know the new total mass or not. Both are rearrangements of the same relationship.

Added Mass ÷ New Total Mass = ΔCG ÷ (Old CG − Load Arm)
Use when you KNOW the new total mass
Added Mass ÷ Old Mass = ΔCG ÷ (New CG − Load Arm)
Use when you DON'T know the new total mass
Added Mass
Mass to Add/Remove
The mass being added (or removed) from aircraft
ΔCG
CG Change
How far the CG needs to shift
Load Arm
Position of Added Mass
Where the new mass is placed
CG Position

Depends on which formula you use
💡
Quick tip: If you don't know the new mass, use Old Mass with New CG. If you know the new mass, use New Mass with Old CG.

✏️ WORKED EXAMPLE

Question

Aircraft has TOM of 3,800 lb with CG at 95 inches (out of rear limit). How much ballast in the forward cargo (at 22.5 inches) will move CG to 93 inches?

Old Mass: 3,800 lb
Old CG: 95 in
Target CG: 93 in
Load Arm: 22.5 in
1
We don't know new mass, so use: Mass ÷ Old Mass = ΔCG ÷ (New CG − Load Arm)
ΔCG = 95 − 93 = 2 inches
2
Calculate (New CG − Load Arm)
93 − 22.5 = 70.5 inches
3
Rearrange: Mass = (ΔCG ÷ Distance) × Old Mass
Mass = (2 ÷ 70.5) × 3,800 = 107.8 lb
ANSWER
≈ 108 lb of ballast needed

Cargo Hold Limits
Floor Loading (kg/m²)
Floor Load = Mass ÷ Area
Weight per unit area — measured in kg/m² or lb/ft²
The Triangle Method

Use the cover-up triangle: put Mass on top, Floor Load (FL) and Area (A) on the bottom. Cover what you need — the remaining arrangement gives you the formula. Bottom items multiply; top divides by bottom.

Question

A container has floor contact area of 1.44 m². The cargo compartment has a max floor load of 732 kg/m². What is the maximum mass that can be loaded?

Area: 1.44 m²
Max FL: 732 kg/m²
1
We need Mass — cover M in the triangle, get FL × A
Mass = FL × A = 732 × 1.44 = 1,054 kg

Linear Weight Distribution
Running Load (kg/m)
Running Load = Mass ÷ Length
Weight per unit length along the aircraft's longitudinal axis
RL
Running Load
Measured in kg/m — weight spread along floor length
Mass
Object Weight
Total mass of the item being loaded
Length
Along Aircraft Axis
The dimension running nose-to-tail
How is this different from Floor Loading?

Floor Loading = kg/m² (weight per area). Running Load = kg/m (weight per length along the aircraft axis). Running load matters because aircraft floors have structural supports running across the fuselage — the load is distributed along these supports.

BOX
Aircraft Longitudinal Axis →
💡
Key insight: The LONGER the side along the axis, the LOWER the running load. Spread the weight over more floor length to stay within limits.

✏️ WORKED EXAMPLE

Question

A 300 kg box measures 1.0m × 1.5m × 0.6m. Max running load is 257.9 kg/m. Which side should be placed along the aircraft axis?

Mass: 300 kg
Dimensions: 1.0 × 1.5 × 0.6 m
Limit: 257.9 kg/m
1
Try 1.0m side along axis
RL = 300 ÷ 1.0 = 300 kg/m → EXCEEDS LIMIT ✗
2
Try 1.5m side along axis
RL = 300 ÷ 1.5 = 200 kg/m → WITHIN LIMIT ✓
3
Try 0.6m side along axis
RL = 300 ÷ 0.6 = 500 kg/m → EXCEEDS LIMIT ✗
ANSWER
Place the 1.5m side along the axis

Load Sheet Indices
Dry Operating Index (DOI)
DOI = DOM Moment ÷ Constant
A scaled-down moment for easier load sheet calculations
DOI = (DOM × CG Arm) ÷ 12,000
Example using 12,000 as the scaling constant
Why use Index instead of Moment?

Raw moments can be enormous numbers — imagine 50,000 kg × 15m = 750,000 kg·m. Dividing by a constant (like 12,000) gives smaller, easier numbers to work with on load sheets. The DOI is the index for the CG position at Dry Operating Mass. Similarly, you'll see Loading Index (LI) for added items.

⚠️
DOI ≠ DOM: DOI is an index (scaled moment), DOM is a mass. Don't confuse them!

10
Flight Characteristics
Effects of CG Position
Why CG Limits Matter

The CG position directly affects stability and control. Operating outside CG limits can make the aircraft uncontrollable. Always verify CG is within the certified envelope for all phases of flight — takeoff, cruise, and landing.

◀ CG TOO FAR FORWARD
  • Nose heavy — needs more elevator to pitch up
  • Higher stall speed
  • Harder to rotate on takeoff
  • More stable but less efficient
  • Higher fuel consumption (more trim drag)
  • Longer takeoff run
CG TOO FAR AFT ▶
  • Tail heavy — reduced longitudinal stability
  • Risk of uncontrollable pitch up
  • Easier to over-rotate
  • Lower stall speed but harder to recover
  • May become uncontrollable in stall
  • Can lead to loss of aircraft
🎯
Direction Rules:
• Add mass FORWARD → CG moves FORWARD
• Add mass AFT → CG moves AFT
• Move mass AFT → CG moves AFT
• Remove mass FORWARD → CG moves AFT
