01
Earth Model
Shape
Oblate Spheroid
Earth is squashed at the poles, bulging at the equator. The WGS-84 ellipsoid models this shape. Compression ratio ≈ 1/298.257
Key Values
Nautical Mile Lengths
Location1' of LatMetres
North Pole (90°)60.31 NM1,862 m
Mid-lat (45°)60.01 NM1,852 m
Equator (0°)59.71 NM1,844 m
Poles = longest NM (flatter surface, gentler curvature, bigger radius of curvature → longer arc per 1'). Equator = shortest. Average = 1,852 m at ~45°.
02
Geodetic vs Geocentric Latitude
GEODETIC
Angle between the normal to the ellipsoid surface and the equatorial plane. This is what charts and GPS use. Always the larger value (except at equator & poles where they're equal).
GEOCENTRIC
Angle between the line to the Earth's centre and the equatorial plane. Always the smaller value. Max difference from geodetic occurs at ~45° latitude.
Geodetic = always ≥ geocentric. They're equal at the equator and poles only. For longitude, geodetic and geocentric give the same value (both measured in the equatorial plane).
03
Meridian Convergence
Convergence = Δ Longitude × sin(Latitude)
Meridians converge toward the poles. At the equator, convergence = 0° (meridians are parallel). At the poles, convergence = Δlong itself. This matters for great circle track direction changes between two points.
For a great circle track, the initial track and final track differ by the convergence angle. The track at the midpoint = the mean of initial and final tracks.
01
Great Circle Distance
Cosine Rule for Spherical Triangles
The Big Formula
cos(d) = sin(lat₁) × sin(lat₂) + cos(lat₁) × cos(lat₂) × cos(Δlong)
Distance (NM) = d × 60
Take arccos of the result to get d in degrees, then multiply by 60 (1° of great circle = 60 NM). Watch the signs: opposite hemispheres → one latitude is negative.
02
Departure (East-West Distance)
Finding Distance
Departure Formula
Departure (NM) = Δ Long (') × cos(lat)
Distance along a parallel of latitude (east-west). The cos(lat) factor accounts for meridians converging toward the poles.
Finding Longitude Change
Rearranged
Δ Long (') = Departure (NM) / cos(lat)
Use this when you know the distance flown east/west and need to find the new longitude. Don't forget to convert minutes back to degrees (÷ 60).
🌍
Quick check: At equator (cos 0° = 1), 1° long = 60 NM. At 60°N (cos 60° = 0.5), 1° long = 30 NM. At 87°N (cos 87° = 0.052), 1° long ≈ 3.14 NM.
03
Parallel Circumference
Two Aircraft Meeting Problem
Full Circle at a Given Latitude
Circumference (NM) = 360 × 60 × cos(lat) = 21,600 × cos(lat)
For "two aircraft flying opposite directions along a parallel" questions: calculate circumference, add both speeds, then Time = Distance ÷ Combined Speed.
Don't confuse the 360 (degrees) with minutes. The full formula is 360° × 60'/° = 21,600' first, THEN × cos(lat).
04
Along a Meridian (North-South)
Distance (NM) = Δ Latitude in minutes
Flying due north or south along a meridian: 1' of latitude = 1 NM. No cosine correction needed — meridians are all great circles of equal length. Just convert the latitude change to minutes.
01
The 1-in-60 Rule
Master Formula
1 NM at 60 NM = 1°
Height (ft) = Angle (°) × Distance (ft) / 60
Distance (ft) = Height (ft) × 60 / Angle (°)
Works for any small angle (below ~15°). This is the foundation for lateral track error, descent planning, and gradient calculations. The number 60 is always the constant.
02
Gradient — Two Ways
DEGREES
Gradient (°) = (Δ Height / 100) ÷ Distance (NM)
Divide height in feet by 100, then divide by NM.

Example: 27,500 ft over 75 NM
= (27,500 / 100) / 75 = 3.7°
PERCENTAGE
Gradient (%) = (Δ Height / Distance in ft) × 100
Both values must be in feet. Then × 100.

Example: 27,500 ft over 75 NM
= (27,500 / 455,700) × 100 = 6.03%
🎯
Sanity check: Degrees is always a smaller number than percentage. A 3° ILS glideslope ≈ 5% gradient. If your answer comes out backwards, you used the wrong formula.
03
Descent Planning
Top of Descent
Distance to Start Descent
Distance (ft) = (Δ Height × 60) / Glidepath angle (°)
Convert result from feet to NM (÷ 6,076).
Example: 3,500 ft to lose at 3° = (3500 × 60) / 3 = 70,000 ft = ~11.5 NM
Time in Descent
Duration
Time = Δ Height / Rate of Descent
Example: 3,500 ft at 750 ft/min = 4.67 min = 4 min 40 sec
04
Height at a Given Distance
Method 1 — 1-in-60
Approximate
Height = Distance (ft) × Angle / 60
Example: 24,320 ft from threshold at 3°
= 24,320 × 3 / 60 = 1,216 ft AGL
Method 2 — Trigonometry
Exact
Height = tan(angle) × Distance (ft)
Example: tan(3°) × 24,320
= 0.0524 × 24,320 = 1,274 ft AGL
The two methods give slightly different answers (1,216 vs 1,274 ft). Use whichever the question specifies. Also watch for the altitude vs height trap — if they ask for altitude, add airport elevation!

CAS → TAS Conversion
Step-by-Step Procedure
Airspeed Window Method
StepActionWindow
1Set pressure altitude against OATAIR SPEED
2Find CAS on inner → read raw TAS on outerAIR SPEED
3Calculate comp correction: TAS/100 − 3COMP. CORR.
4Move inner disc LEFT by that many divisionsCOMP. CORR.
5Re-read CAS on inner → corrected TAS on outerAIR SPEED
Example: FL410, OAT −42°C, CAS 220 kt → Raw TAS 460 kt → Comp corr = 460/100 − 3 = 1.6 divisions left → Corrected TAS = 443 kt
NEVER use the compressibility factor given in the question text. Always use the CRP-5's built-in COMP. CORR. window — they give you a value to trick you.
02
TAS → Mach Conversion
StepAction
1Rotate inner disc so Mach index arrow appears in AIRSPEED window
2Set Mach index against OAT
3Find TAS on outer scale → read Mach number on inner scale
Example: TAS 443 kt, OAT −42°C → Mach 0.75 (shown as "7.5" on the scale)
💡
Compressibility correction only matters at high TAS (above ~300 kt). It always reduces TAS — the uncorrected value overshoots because air compresses at high speed.
01
Wind Triangle Convention
Vector Arrow Convention
→
1 arrow — Heading / TAS (air vector)
→→
2 arrows — Track / Groundspeed (ground vector)
→→→
3 arrows — Wind direction / Wind speed (wind vector)
Vector addition (tip-to-tail): Place the tail of the second vector at the tip of the first. The resultant goes from the tail of the first to the tip of the second.

Wind triangle: Heading vector + Wind vector = Track vector. The wind "pushes" you from heading to track.
02
Key Relationships
Drift
Track vs Heading
Track = Heading ± Drift
Wind from the left → drift right → Track > Heading (add drift). Wind from right → drift left → Track < Heading (subtract).

Memory: "Wind pushes your track downwind"
Groundspeed
TAS vs GS
GS = TAS ± Wind Component
Headwind → GS < TAS (subtract).
Tailwind → GS > TAS (add).

GS is what you actually cover over the ground per hour.
03
CDMVT — The Compass Chain
Compass ± Deviation → Magnetic ± Variation → True
East is least (subtract from True to get Magnetic), West is best (add).

Or going the other direction (C → T): East = ADD, West = SUBTRACT.

For VOR → use variation at the station.
For NDB → use variation at the aircraft.
01
Chart Projections
Mercator
Normal Mercator
Rhumb lines = straight lines
Great circles = curved toward the equator
Scale expands with latitude (cos factor)
Correct at equator or standard parallel
Used for: equatorial charts, plotting charts
Lambert
Lambert Conformal Conic
Great circles ≈ straight lines (nearly)
Rhumb lines = curved toward the equator
Scale correct at 2 standard parallels
Convergence = Δlong × sin(parallel of origin) = constant of the cone (n)
Used for: en-route charts, Jeppesen airways
Polar Stereo
Polar Stereographic
Great circles ≈ straight lines
Meridians = straight lines radiating from pole
Convergence = Δ longitude itself
Used for: polar routes, above ~80° latitude
02
Scale
Scale = Chart Distance / Earth Distance
Example: 1:500,000 means 1 cm on chart = 500,000 cm (5 km) on Earth.

Mercator scale at a given latitude:
Scale at lat = Scale at equator × (1 / cos lat)
03
Time & Arc Conversion
Arc to Time
Longitude → Time
360° = 24 hours
15° = 1 hour
1° = 4 minutes
1' = 4 seconds
East = ahead in time, West = behind

LMT = UTC + (longitude / 15) for East
LMT = UTC − (longitude / 15) for West
Sunrise/Sunset
Time Calculations
Sunrise/sunset times in the Air Almanac are given for specific latitudes in UTC at the Greenwich meridian.

To find sunrise/sunset at your longitude: take the tabulated time and add/subtract the arc-to-time conversion of your longitude.

East → sunrise earlier (subtract)
West → sunrise later (add)
⏰
Quick mental math: 90°E = 6 hours ahead of UTC. 45°W = 3 hours behind UTC. Just divide longitude by 15.
