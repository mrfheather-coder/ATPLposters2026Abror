📡 NDB / ADF LF/MF · 190–1750 kHz
NDB Types
  • En-route NDBs — high power, used for airways navigation, range up to 250 NM
  • Locator beacons — low power, used for airfield/approach, range 10–25 NM
  • Locators often co-located with ILS outer marker (LOM) or middle marker (LMM)
  • NDBs are different from marker beacons — locators provide directional guidance TO the approach fix
Modulation Types
  • A1A — Carrier with keyed Morse. Carrier switches ON/OFF. BFO needed to hear ident. Bearing erratic during ident
  • A2A — Carrier with AM Morse. Tone audible without BFO. Bearing stable during ident
  • A3E — Carrier with AM speech (VHF-COM)
  • First symbol indicates modulation type of main carrier
  • In A1A keying, amplitude and frequency both remain constant
ADF Instruments
  • RBI (Relative Bearing Indicator) — fixed card, 000° always at top, shows relative bearing only. Must add heading: QDM = HDG + RB
  • RMI (Radio Magnetic Indicator) — rotating card driven by flux valve, shows magnetic bearing directly. Just read head (QDM) and tail (QDR)
  • RMI = no math needed. RBI = must calculate
  • For IAA exams: no deviation correction on RMI (flux valve gives magnetic heading directly)

NDB Errors
  • Dip error — caused by banking. ADF antenna tilts, needle goes erratic during turns. Key word: "rapidly moves upon banking"
  • Quadrantal error — aircraft metallic structure re-radiates signal. Steady error at 045°/135°/225°/315° RB. Always present, not related to banking
  • Mountain effect (Diffraction) — long NDB waves bend around mountains, causing bearing errors
  • Coastal refraction — wave changes speed crossing coastline (faster over sea, slower over land). Worst at acute angles to coast
  • Night effect (Skywave) — ionospheric reflection at night causes bearing errors
  • Absorption — terrain absorbs ground wave energy, reducing range. Why transmitter power matters
Accuracy & Homing vs Tracking
  • ADF accuracy by day: ±5°
  • VDF accuracy: ±2°
  • Homing — keep needle on nose (RB 000°), no wind correction, curved path, works TO the NDB only
  • Tracking — apply WCA for straight ground track, works TO or FROM the NDB
Wave Propagation Errors
NDB → Diffraction (bends around obstacles)
VOR → Reflection (bounces off obstacles)
NDB-D, VOR-R — D before R, like NDB before VOR
📏 DME UHF · 960–1215 MHz
Operating Principle
  • Aircraft sends interrogation pulse on UHF
  • Ground station receives, waits 50 μs delay, replies
  • Aircraft measures round trip time → calculates distance
  • Each aircraft uses unique random jitter pattern to identify its own replies
  • Maximum capacity: 100 aircraft (~2700 pulse pairs/sec ÷ 27 per aircraft)
  • When saturated, strongest signals (closest aircraft) prioritised
Slant Range

DME measures slant range (diagonal), not ground distance. Error worst when close and high.

Slant Range Formula
Slant Range = √(ground distance² + height²)

Negligible when distance > 3× height
DME Line of Position
On a chart, DME LOP is plotted as a circle at ground distance, centred on the DME station (not aircraft)
Distance Calculation
DME Distance Formula
1. Subtract ground delay: time − 50 μs
2. Convert to km: result ÷ 3.33 μs/km
3. Convert to NM: km ÷ 1.852
4. Divide by 2 (round trip)

Key numbers: 50 μs delay · 3.33 μs per km · 1.852 km/NM

DME Groundspeed
  • DME calculates groundspeed from rate of range change — not actual groundspeed
  • Only accurate when flying directly toward or away from station
  • On a DME arc: groundspeed reads ~0 kts because slant range isn't changing
  • Descending on a DME arc: slant range remains essentially constant, so GS still reads ~0
Frequency Pairing & Ident
  • DME (UHF) is automatically paired with VOR/ILS (VHF) — one tuning action
  • 63 MHz offset between interrogation and reply frequencies
  • Pulse spacing: 12 μs (X channels), 36 μs (Y channels)

Co-located ident cycle (40 sec):

  • VOR ident × 3 at 1020 Hz (lower pitch)
  • DME ident × 1 at 1350 Hz (higher pitch)
Special Features
  • Echo Protection Circuit (EPC) — ignores reflected/multipath signals, tracks only the first (direct) reply
  • Memory mode — if signal interrupted (antenna masking), holds last distance for 10–15 seconds before going to search mode
  • TACAN — military system. Bearing = military only. DME = usable by civilians. VORTAC = VOR + TACAN co-located
✈️ ILS LOC: VHF 108–112 MHz · GP: UHF 329–335 MHz
Components & Coverage
  • Localizer — far end of runway, lateral guidance
  • Coverage: ±10° to 25 NM · ±35° to 17 NM
  • Full scale deflection = 2.5° off centerline
  • Glideslope — side of runway, vertical guidance
  • Coverage: ±8° to 10 NM minimum
  • Full scale deflection = 0.7° off glidepath
  • Beam thickness: 1.4° (0.7° either side)
How ILS Works — DDM

ILS uses Amplitude Modulation with two tones: 90 Hz and 150 Hz. Position determined by Difference in Depth of Modulation (DDM).

  • Left of centerline → 90 Hz dominates → fly right
  • Right of centerline → 150 Hz dominates → fly left
  • On centerline → equal modulation (40% each) → DDM = 0
System Measurement Methods
ILS → Depth (DDM) · VOR → Phase · MLS → Time · DME → Time (round trip)
Glideslope Details
  • Standard angle: 3° (range 2.5°–3.5°)
  • Vertical coverage: lower 0.45 × θ, upper 1.75 × θ
  • For 3°: coverage 1.35° to 5.25°
  • False glideslope at ~2–3× θ (6°–9°) with reversed sense
  • Always intercept from below to avoid false GS
Rate of Descent
ROD = (GS angle × Groundspeed × 100) ÷ 60
Shortcut for 3°: ROD ≈ GS × 5

ILS Signal Issues
  • Beam bends — slow, gentle deviations. Normal and expected. Aircraft CAN follow. Checked by flight tests
  • Scalloping — rapid, erratic needle movements. Aircraft CANNOT follow. Don't chase — fly stable approach
  • FM Immunity Filter — protects localizer only from FM radio interference (FM radio band 87.5–108 MHz is adjacent to LOC 108–112 MHz)

Protected Areas:

  • Critical area — vehicles/aircraft completely EXCLUDED during all ILS ops
  • Sensitive area — vehicles/aircraft CONTROLLED during CAT II/III ops
  • Coverage area — obstacles monitored, can cause multipath
Back Course & Cross-Check
  • Back course — signal from back of localizer antenna, non-precision (no glideslope)
  • Causes reverse sensing on CDI — set front course heading on HSI or use BC mode to correct
  • GS cross-check: verify correct glideslope by comparing baro-altitude vs DME distance against published chart values
  • Continuation bias — dangerous tendency to continue approach when conditions say go around
🎯 MLS SHF · 5031–5091 MHz
Overview
  • Designed to replace ILS — technically superior but killed by GPS/GNSS
  • Peak deployment: ~200 systems worldwide, now almost none
  • Uses Time Reference Scanning Beam (TRSB) principle
  • SHF band: 5031–5090.7 MHz, vertically polarised
  • 200 channels available, 300 kHz separation
  • Can be sited in hilly terrain — no need for flat, leveled ground
Ground Components

Three separate components:

  • Azimuth transmitter — far end of runway, scans left-right, lateral guidance. Coverage: ±40° to 20 NM
  • Elevation transmitter — side of runway, scans up-down, vertical guidance. Glideslopes 0.9°–20° selectable
  • DME facility (DME/P) — precision distance, NOT marker beacons

Back azimuth: ±20° of runway direction, up to 15° elevation, 10 NM range, 10,000 ft height

How TRSB Works
  • Narrow beam sweeps "TO" (left→right), then "FRO" (right→left)
  • Aircraft measures time between TO and FRO scans
  • Time difference = exact angular position
  • Same principle for elevation: beam scans up then down
  • Result: precise 3D position anywhere within coverage volume
Advantages Over ILS
  • Curved & segmented approaches — not limited to one straight path
  • Selectable glideslope angle — pilot chooses descent angle
  • 200 channels vs ILS 40 — no frequency congestion
  • Hilly terrain siting — no flat ground required
  • Minimal multipath — scanning beam can be interrupted to avoid reflections from stationary objects
  • Higher frequency (SHF) — reduces ground reflection effects
Cockpit Operation
  • Tuning: select channel number (not frequency)
  • Select glideslope angle for approach
  • Display: 2D presentation (same CDI cross-hairs as ILS)
  • Approach type: 3D segmented (multiple straight segments joined at different angles)
MLS can minimise multipath errors because the transmission can be interrupted to avoid reflection by stationary objects — the scanning beam is blanked as it passes known reflective obstacles

🛡️ SSR / Transponder Interrogation: 1030 MHz · Reply: 1090 MHz
Interrogation Pulses
  • P1 — main interrogation pulse (directional antenna)
  • P2 — side lobe suppression (omnidirectional, 2 μs after P1). If P2 ≥ P1, aircraft is in sidelobe → no reply
  • P3 — mode-defining pulse. Spacing from P1 determines mode:
  • 8 μs = Mode A (identity/squawk)
  • 21 μs = Mode C (altitude)
  • P4 — Mode S control pulse:
  • 0.8 μs (short) = Mode A/C only reply, Mode S stays quiet
  • 1.6 μs (long) = Mode S replies with 24-bit address
Modes Explained
  • Mode A — aircraft identity. 4-digit octal squawk code (0–7 per digit = 4096 codes)
  • Mode C — pressure altitude in 100 ft increments from encoding altimeter
  • Mode S — Selective interrogation. Each aircraft has unique 24-bit address (16.7 million unique codes). Enables private ground-to-air data link

Ground station alternates Mode A and C interrogations rapidly (~1200/sec). Processor combines all replies into one unified display for controller.

Mode S Operation
  • 1. All-Call — ground sends P4 long (1.6 μs), all transponders reply. Mode S aircraft reply with 24-bit address. Ground "discovers" new aircraft.
  • 2. Lockout — known Mode S aircraft told to stop replying to all-calls. 18-second lockout timer. Reduces congestion.
  • 3. Roll-Call — ground selectively interrogates individual aircraft by their 24-bit address. Private conversation, no interference.
Mode S = S for Selective
Like a teacher taking attendance once (all-call), then asking individual students by name (roll-call)

GPS vs GLONASS Differences
Different frequencies, signal encoding (GPS=CDMA, GLONASS=FDMA), datum (GPS=WGS-84, GLONASS=PZ-90), time reference. But interoperable — a suitably equipped aircraft can use both simultaneously.
How GPS Works
  • Each satellite broadcasts unique PRN code with precise timestamp (atomic clock)
  • Receiver compares sent time vs received time
  • Time difference × speed of light = pseudo-range (includes clock error, hence "pseudo")
  • 4th satellite needed to solve receiver clock error
  • GPS is one-way, passive — receiver only listens, never transmits. Unlimited users.
  • Position = time measurement. Groundspeed = Doppler shift of carrier frequency
Minimum Satellites Required
3 SVs → 2D fix (lat/long only)
4 SVs → 3D fix (lat/long/alt + clock)
5 SVs → 3D + RAIM fault detection
6 SVs → 3D + RAIM + FDE (fault detection & exclusion)

Dual Frequency Advantage
Using both L1 and L2 eliminates ionospheric delay only. Cannot fix tropospheric delay (same effect on all frequencies). Ionosphere = dispersive. Troposphere = non-dispersive.
GPS Time & Navigation Message

Satellites use GPS Time — independent time system, started Jan 6 1980, NO leap seconds (unlike UTC). Receiver converts to UTC using UTC parameters in nav message.

Navigation message contains 6 items:

  • Almanac data — rough position of ALL satellites
  • Ephemeris — precise position of transmitting satellite
  • Satellite clock corrections — fix timing drift
  • UTC parameters — convert GPS Time to UTC
  • Ionospheric model — signal delay corrections
  • Satellite health data — use/don't use status
GNSS Errors (UERE)

UERE (User Equivalent Range Error) = sum of ALL errors affecting pseudo-range:

  • Ionospheric delay — greatest error source, signal slows through charged particles, varies signal path length
  • Tropospheric delay — signal slows through lower atmosphere
  • Satellite clock error — atomic clock drift
  • Ephemeris error — satellite not exactly where reported
  • Multipath — signal bounces off surfaces
  • Receiver noise — receiver hardware imprecision
Position Accuracy Formula
Actual Position Error = UERE × GDOP

UERE = all range measurement errors
GDOP = geometry multiplier (NOT part of UERE)
DOP amplifies or reduces UERE errors
GDOP — Dilution of Precision

Best geometry: 1 satellite directly overhead + 3 near horizon, 120° apart in azimuth. Maximum 3D volume = lowest DOP = best accuracy.

DOP Family:

  • GDOP — Geometric (position + time) — the complete picture
  • PDOP — Position (lat + long + alt)
  • HDOP — Horizontal (lat + long)
  • VDOP — Vertical (altitude)
  • TDOP — Time (clock)

Low DOP = good (satellites spread). High DOP = bad (satellites clustered). GPS coverage varies with time as satellites constantly move.

EGNOS broadcast contains:

  • Integrity message — use/don't use for each GPS satellite (within 6 seconds)
  • Wide area DGPS corrections — clock, orbit, ephemeris fixes
  • Ionospheric delay model — delay correction for your location
  • GEO satellite ephemeris/clock — so GEO acts as bonus ranging satellite

RAIM — Receiver Autonomous Integrity Monitoring
  • Receiver — happens in YOUR aircraft GPS receiver
  • Autonomous — does it BY ITSELF, no external help
  • Integrity — checks if GPS data is TRUSTWORTHY
  • Monitoring — constantly watching

Uses redundant measurements from extra moving GNSS satellites (NOT geostationary). Calculates position using different combinations and checks if answers agree.

  • 5 SVs — can detect fault but NOT identify which satellite
  • 6 SVs — can detect AND exclude the faulty satellite
RAIM always involves the aircraft receiver using extra satellites. Any exam answer mentioning ground stations, satellite self-checks, or PRN codes is WRONG.
GBAS / GLS Approaches
  • GBAS = the system. GLS = the approach procedure (like ILS = system, ILS approach = procedure)
  • Tuned via 5-digit channel number (not frequency)
  • Corrections via VHF datalink (VDB)
  • Aircraft needs GBAS-capable receiver (MMR)
  • FAS data block defines approach path: threshold position, course, glidepath angle, crossing height
  • Provides lateral AND vertical guidance
  • Currently supports down to 200 ft minimums (CAT I equivalent)
  • More precise than ILS in terminal area — sub-1m accuracy everywhere within coverage

🧭 PBN — Performance Based Navigation RNAV + RNP Specifications
What is PBN?

PBN is the umbrella framework for defining navigation requirements based on performance (how accurate) rather than equipment (what specific box).

PBN is NOT sensor-specific. It doesn't care what equipment you use — only that you meet the accuracy requirement.

  • Old way: "You MUST have a VOR receiver"
  • PBN way: "You must be accurate to within X NM — we don't care how"

PBN has two families: RNAV and RNP

TSE — Total System Error
TSE Formula
TSE = PDE + NSE + FTE

TSE (EPE) must be ≤ required nav spec
  • PDE (Path Definition Error) — database path vs desired path don't match. Assumed ZERO
  • NSE (Navigation System Error) — difference between estimated and actual position (sensor errors)
  • FTE (Flight Technical Error) — crew/autopilot ability to follow track. Must not exceed HALF of RNP value
Since PDE = 0
TSE = NSE + FTE in practice
Example RNP 1: FTE must not exceed 0.5 NM

3D Approach Operations

3D = lateral guidance + vertical guidance. Must have BOTH to be 3D.

  • GNSS + baro-VNAV → 3D ✅
  • GLS (GBAS) → 3D ✅
  • ILS (LOC + GS) → 3D ✅
  • SBAS (LPV) → 3D ✅
  • RNP 1 → 2D ❌ (terminal nav spec, lateral only)
  • LNAV only → 2D ❌ (no vertical guidance)

RNAV SID vs Overlay SID
  • Conventional SID — designed using ground navaids (VOR, NDB, DME). Route goes beacon to beacon.
  • RNAV SID — designed from scratch for RNAV/GPS. Waypoints anywhere, more efficient, shorter routes.
  • RNAV Overlay SID — takes existing conventional SID and allows RNAV aircraft to fly the SAME track using FMS/GPS instead of tuning ground navaids. Same route, different navigation method.
Overlay = Bridge Solution
Old aircraft follow VORs. RNAV aircraft follow FMS. Same track, same separation, everyone coexists. Eventually replaced by proper RNAV SIDs.
Parallel Offset & Database Rules
  • Parallel offset — fly parallel to airway at fixed distance. If aircraft turns more than 90°, RNAV system automatically discontinues the offset
  • Missing waypoint in database — for anything tighter than RNAV 5, you CANNOT manually create it. Must request alternative procedure from ATC.
  • Route modification — pilots can insert/delete waypoints from the active flight plan per ATC clearance. NEVER modify the navigation database itself.
  • Manual waypoint entry — allowed ONLY for RNAV 5. All tighter specs require validated database waypoints.
Baro-VNAV & IRS Alignment
  • Baro-VNAV approach — uses barometric altimeter for vertical guidance = 3D approach. Crew must cross-check both pilots' barometric altimeters agree before approach.
  • IRS alignment — approximately 10 minutes at mid latitudes. Faster at equator (stronger horizontal rotation component). Slower/impossible at poles.
  • IRS capability time — can be extended by updating with external position data (GNSS, VOR/DME fixes). Resets the drift.
